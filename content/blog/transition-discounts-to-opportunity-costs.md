# What is IMU Pricing? The Markup Method Every Billion-Dollar Brand Uses

## The definition

IMU pricing — Initial Markup pricing — is the retail method of building a selling price from cost of goods, a profit markup, and a stack of allowances for shrink, financing, shipping, discounts, and market adjustment. The allowances absorb promotional and operational costs so the profit markup stays whole when you flex price. Every billion-dollar brand prices this way. Every. Single. One.

## How IMU pricing works

The formula is simple addition:

**COGS \+ Profit Markup \+ Allowances \= Selling Price**

Expanded:

**COGS \+ PMU \+ Shrink \+ Financing \+ Shipping \+ Discounts \+ Market Adjustment \= Selling Price**

Each component does a specific job.

**COGS (Cost of Goods Sold)** is the landed cost of inventory in your warehouse — manufacturing, freight, tariffs, duties, fees. Everything that happened to the unit before you owned it.

**PMU (Profit Markup)** is the per-unit markup that covers fixed operating expenses, variable operating expenses, and the net profit you planned. This is the number you protect.

The five allowances exist because every retailer pays these costs whether they price for them or not. Pricing for them moves the cost from your P\&L to the customer's receipt.

- **Shrink** — lost, stolen, damaged inventory. The cost is real. Either it lives in the selling price or it eats your profit.  
- **Financing** — card processing, BNPL fees. 2% to 10% depending on the rail. The customer used the service; price for it.  
- **Shipping** — calculated from weight, dimensions, and zones. If you ever plan to say "free shipping," it has to live here.  
- **Discounts** — the allowance the DTC bros pretend isn't on the spreadsheet. It's the lever that lets you meet different buyers at different prices.  
- **Market Adjustment** — rounds the price up to the nearest $9, or matches a competitor without cutting the markup. Allow for it; don't absorb it.

## Why IMU pricing matters now

The DTC playbook of the 2010s — clean pricing, no discounts, brand purity — broke. Customer acquisition cost is up roughly 60% since 2020 across paid social, and consumer real wages have softened enough that the same product hits three different income tiers as three different price points. \[External citation slot: NRF retail shrink data \+ Fed BNPL transaction cost data.\]

If you price without a discount allowance, every promotion is a direct deduction from the profit markup. Run a 20% promo on a flat-priced item and you've handed away most of your margin. Build the allowance in and the same 20% promo is just the allowance being spent the way you planned it.

The "discount spiral" is real, but it isn't caused by discounting. It's caused by pricing without allowances, then discovering you need to discount anyway, then having no markup left to absorb it. By the time the spiral is visible, the pricing decision was made twelve months earlier.

## How IMU pricing differs from cost-plus and keystone pricing

Cost-plus pricing adds a fixed margin percentage to COGS. It treats every dollar of revenue as if it carries the same cost structure. It doesn't price for shrink, doesn't price for financing, and treats every discount as a margin hit. Cost-plus works for B2B contract pricing. It breaks in consumer retail the moment you run a promotion.

Keystone pricing doubles COGS to get the selling price. It's an approximation of IMU without the allowance discipline. Keystone assumes a 50% gross margin covers everything — which is true at $10M in revenue and false at $500K, because the fixed-cost burden per unit is different at every scale.

IMU pricing differs by separating the profit markup from the cost absorbers. Allowances flex. The profit markup doesn't. That separation is what lets the same SKU sell at four different price points to four different cohorts without anyone in finance flinching.

## How to apply IMU pricing to your store

1. **Calculate landed COGS per SKU.** Manufacturing plus freight plus tariffs plus duties plus warehousing-in. Not the invoice from the supplier — the full landed number.  
2. **Set PMU based on your operating budget.** Add fixed opex, variable opex, and target net profit, then divide by planned unit volume. That's the dollar markup per unit, not a percentage.  
3. **Add the five allowances as line items.** Don't guess. Pull shrink from inventory reconciliation, financing from payment processor statements, shipping from your 3PL invoice, discounts from the discount rate you intend to run (start with 20%), and market adjustment from competitive shop-along data.

Now you have a selling price. The chorus sings: *no one will pay that price.*

Yes and no. In a portfolio of 85M U.S. buyers, the cohorts broadly split like this:

- The top 20% of households pay the selling price. They value their time more than a $17 discount.  
- The middle 40% pay 70%–90% of the selling price. They look for offers, they use the signup discount, they're price-aware but not price-driven.  
- The bottom 40% pay close to COGS \+ PMU. They may eat into the profit allowance a bit. They're still profitable because the unit cost falls as volume rises across the whole portfolio.

That's cross-subsidization. The top 20% partially subsidizes the unit cost for the other 80%, and the profits from the top tier offset thinner profits at the bottom. The whole is greater than the sum of the parts. This only works if the allowances were in the price to begin with.

Here's how it looks in action. A $74.99 unit priced with full IMU, sold two units per transaction, flexed across discount tiers:

## FAQ

**Q: What does IMU stand for in retail pricing?** A: IMU stands for Initial Markup. It's the markup applied to the cost of goods at the moment a product is first priced for sale, before any discounts, markdowns, or allowances are subtracted at the point of transaction. IMU is calculated as a dollar amount per unit, not a percentage, because percentages obscure the underlying cost structure.

**Q: How is IMU pricing different from gross margin?** A: Gross margin is a backward-looking percentage calculated after sales close. IMU pricing is the forward-looking method that builds the selling price in the first place. Gross margin tells you what happened; IMU pricing tells you what should happen. A retailer can have a healthy IMU and still report poor gross margin if discounts exceeded the discount allowance.

**Q: Why include a discount allowance if you don't plan to discount?** A: Because you will. Every retailer runs welcome offers, abandoned-cart recovery, seasonal promotions, and clearance. If those weren't planned for at the pricing stage, every one of them comes out of profit. Allowances let you run the promotions you were going to run anyway without surprising the P\&L.

**Q: What's a typical discount allowance for a Shopify store doing $1M–$5M?** A: Most stores in that band carry 15%–25% as a discount allowance, depending on category. Apparel and accessories sit at the high end. Consumables and tightly-priced commodity goods sit lower. The right number is whatever your actual discount rate has been over the trailing 12 months, plus a small buffer.

**Q: Does IMU pricing work for products with high price sensitivity?** A: Yes — that's exactly where it works hardest. Price-sensitive categories are where the discount allowance gets used most aggressively to flex price across cohorts. The allowance gives you headroom to meet the price-driven 40% of buyers without abandoning the price-insensitive 20%.

**Q: How do I know my allowances are sized right?** A: Pull the trailing 12 months of actual costs in each category — actual shrink, actual processing fees, actual shipping spend, actual discount dollars handed out — and compare to what's in the price. If the allowance was smaller than the cost, the gap came out of profit. If it was larger, you have room to compete on price. Reset annually.

> **Summary**  
> 

> - IMU pricing builds COGS, profit markup, and five allowances into the selling price. The allowances are a shield around the markup.  
> - The discount spiral isn't real — it's the symptom of pricing without a discount allowance and being forced to discount anyway.  
> - Cross-subsidization across income tiers only works when allowances were priced in at the start.
